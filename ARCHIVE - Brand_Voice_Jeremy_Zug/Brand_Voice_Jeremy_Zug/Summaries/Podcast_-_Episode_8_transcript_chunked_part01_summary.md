### Summary:
This episode of "The Claim Game" features an in-depth interview with Kathy Gilbert, a healthcare industry veteran with over 30 years of experience, particularly in provider network management, credentialing, and contracting. Kathy has held senior roles at major payers like Magellan Healthcare and Beacon Health Options (now Carelon) and currently consults for provider organizations, offering a payer-side perspective to demystify complex processes for clinicians.

**Key Insights from Kathy Gilbert:**

1.  **Expert Background & Payer Perspective:**
    *   Kathy's career evolved from direct social work and management in social services to CEO of a residential treatment program, then into contracting for community mental health, eventually leading to significant roles on the payer side with national insurance companies.
    *   She now leverages this extensive payer-side experience to guide provider organizations, especially startups, through the complexities of managed care.

2.  **Contracting & Credentialing Fundamentals:**
    *   These are two interconnected but distinct processes. Payers vary their approach: some credential first, some agree on rates before credentialing, and others do both concurrently, influenced by provider type and the cost of validation.
    *   **CAQH (Council for Affordable Quality Healthcare):** Intended as a central data source for individual practitioners. Its accuracy relies on providers diligently updating their information. Directory validation is an expanded CAQH service, not universally adopted by plans.
    *   **Accreditation Bodies (NCQA & URAC):** Payers adhere to standards from NCQA (National Council on Quality Assurance) and/or URAC (Utilization Review Accreditation Counsel). These accreditations ensure operational quality, consistency, and accountability in processes like credentialing.

3.  **Payer Criteria for Network Providers:**
    *   **Minimum Standards:** Providers must meet baseline requirements (licensure, education, training, insurance, HIPAA compliance).
    *   **Discipline-Specific & Facility Standards:** Unique requirements exist for various mental health disciplines and facility types.
    *   **Network Need & The 80/20 Rule:** Payers assess if there's a genuine need for the provider, considering volume, access gaps, and the observation that ~80% of care is often delivered by ~20% of (high-volume) providers. Lower-volume providers in underserved areas remain crucial.
    *   **Telehealth:** Expanded access but quality monitoring can be challenging without significant patient volume per provider. Administrative performance (clean claims, low complaints) becomes a key indicator for smaller practices.

4.  **CAQH, Ghost Networks & Data Accuracy:**
    *   "Ghost networks" (inaccurate provider directories) are a challenge. Keeping CAQH and direct plan information updated is a dual responsibility of providers and plans.
    *   Legislation (especially tied to Medicare) mandates regular plan outreach for data validation.
    *   Provider non-response to validation requests can lead to removal from directories and, in some states (e.g., California with stringent rules), network disenrollment.

5.  **Managing Caseload & Network Participation:**
    *   Providers can notify plans if their practice is full. Plans usually time-limit this "not accepting new patients" status.
    *   Prolonged "practice full" status with no plan members seen can, rarely, risk network removal if the plan perceives no value.

6.  **Credentialing Timelines & Roadblocks:**
    *   **Typical Timeline:** 90-120 days. Applications (signature attestations) can expire if not processed (120-180 days).
    *   **Processing:** Usually first-in, first-out, but state regulations (e.g., requiring a 40-day response) can prioritize certain applications for national payers (e.g., Magellan, Optum, United, Cigna/Evernorth).
    *   **Centralized Credentialing:** Many large payers have centralized units verifying education, certifications, licensure, background checks, and sanctions.
    *   **Overcoming Roadblocks:** If a plan contact initiated your application, they might assist. Kathy suggests observing the application process if issues arise or consulting internal plan experts/colleagues. Plan representatives generally aim to help, but volume is high, and escalation teams are often small.

7.  **Application Follow-Up Strategy:**
    *   Check plan websites/portals for stated processing times and application status.
    *   Avoid calling before the stated timeframe unless there's a clear issue (e.g., no submission confirmation). Constant calls don't expedite and can divert resources.
    *   Screenshot submission confirmations and save any automated email acknowledgments.
    *   Adding a clinician to an existing group contract is simpler as it often bypasses the full contracting piece, focusing on credentialing.

8.  **Finding Plan Contacts:**
    *   Provider relations team assignments might be on plan websites or in provider manuals.
    *   LinkedIn can be a tool: search for "provider relations," "provider network," or "provider partnership" at specific plans in your state/area. The network management field is relatively small, with professionals often moving between companies.

9.  **Negotiating Contract Terms (Non-Rates):**
    *   **Timely Filing:** Can sometimes be negotiated if unusually short (e.g., 90 days); 180 days is a common upper limit.
    *   **Indemnification Clauses:** Look for reciprocity if the clause is one-sided.
    *   **Notice Requirements:** Provider manuals are often part of the contract. "Silence is acceptance" for changes is common. Facilities might negotiate for required signatures on non-regulatory changes.
    *   **Medicare/Medicaid Addendums:** If not intending to participate, request removal of these addendums.
    *   *Note:* Negotiating language extends the contracting timeline due to legal review. Individual provider contracts are often boilerplate; more negotiation leverage typically exists for large groups/facilities.

10. **Negotiating Rates:**
    *   **Opportunity Exists:** Depends on plan need, provider specialty (e.g., child psychiatry often has leverage), and geographic location (underserved areas).
    *   **Timing:** Unlikely if an increase was granted or the provider joined the network within the last year. Negotiated increases often lock in for 1-2 years.
    *   **Value-Based Contracts:** Require established volume and at least a year of experience with the plan. Can include pay-for-performance (meeting quality standards) or bundled payments.
    *   **Preferred Networks/Incentives:** Some plans offer higher rates for providers in "immediate access networks" (e.g., guaranteeing appointments within 7 days) or "Gold Card" programs for facilities (waiving some auths for consistent high approval/good outcomes).
    *   **Provider Scorecards:** High-volume providers may receive scorecards on metrics like timely claim filing, average sessions, length of stay, admission rates, and post-discharge follow-up (a key HEDIS/NCQA measure is a 7-day follow-up post-higher level of care discharge).

11. **Leveraging Transparency Data:**
    *   Federal regulations require plans to publish contracted rates in machine-readable files, which are difficult for individuals to analyze directly.
    *   Companies (e.g., Tynbil, Serif, Clarify Health) process this data into usable reports.
    *   Kathy analyzes this data for min/max/average rates by CPT code, state, and plan, and identifies common (often MD) rates. She also uses NPI numbers to research competitor rates.
    *   Medicare physician fee schedules (available on the CMS website) serve as another benchmark.

12. **Effective Rate Negotiation Strategies:**
    *   **Data-Driven Approach:** Use transparency data and Medicare rates to support your request.
    *   **Articulate Value:** Clearly define your unique value proposition (e.g., specific populations served, superior outcomes, immediate access, specialized services).
    *   **Understand Plan Needs:** Research if the plan is actively building its network for specific specialties or in certain areas.
    *   **Group Leverage:** Groups often have more negotiating power than solo practitioners.
    *   **Avoid Common Mistakes:** Don't ask too soon after a previous increase, ensure you have supporting data, and understand the plan's perspective.
